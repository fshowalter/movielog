Paul Marsh is a dot-com millionaire. In 2001 that already made him one of the last of his kind, and by the end of this movie he may be the last of a few other kinds too. He's on a yacht off the coast of Spain with his girlfriend, Barbara, when a storm puts the boat on the rocks, and the two of them go ashore to a fishing village called Imboca to look for help. The villagers watch them the way a fisherman watches a full net. Some of them have webbing between their fingers. Nobody in Imboca seems to own an umbrella, though it never stops raining.

If you've read Lovecraft, you'll recognize "The Shadow Over Innsmouth" with a new address. The director is Stuart Gordon, the writer is Dennis Paoli and the producer is Brian Yuzna. Together, they made <span data-imdb-id="tt0089885">_Re-Animator_</span> and <span data-imdb-id="tt0091083">_From Beyond_</span>. Paoli wrote this script in 1985, and it sat in a drawer for fifteen years until a Spanish company put up the money. I assume one condition was that the film be shot where the money lived, so New England became Galicia. The move turns out to be a gift. Imboca's stone streets look older than anything in Massachusetts, the locals mutter in a language Paul doesn't speak, and the church has been rededicated to something you won't find in a hymnal.

Gordon's team has always understood that Lovecraft's stories are about appetites the author couldn't bring himself to name. Lovecraft was a fastidious man who would have walked out of this movie around the second reel. Gordon puts the sleaze back in, and it does what it should: it tells you Imboca stopped following our rules generations ago and has no plans to start again.

Ezra Godden plays Paul as nervous, bespectacled and quick with an exasperated wisecrack. In other words, he plays him the way Jeffrey Combs would have, and I kept missing Combs. Godden even wears a Miskatonic University sweatshirt. Raquel Meroño, a Spanish soap star, plays Barbara, a name I took as a fond nod to Barbara Crampton. It's a sweet gesture and a slightly sad one, like setting a place for a guest who can't come. By 2001, Combs and Crampton had aged out of playing twenty-somethings. Had the Spanish money come in 1987, this might have been something special. 

The best performance comes from Francisco Rabal, who worked for Buñuel in <span data-imdb-id="tt0055601">_Viridiana_</span> and <span data-imdb-id="tt0051983">_Nazarín_</span>. Here he plays Ezequiel, the town drunk, who remembers Imboca before its bargain and tells the story the way a man confesses. It was his last film, and he died the year it came out. He gives the movie a weight it doesn't earn anywhere else.

The makeup effects are first-rate. A scene in which a man is flayed alive belongs with the best practical work in the genre, which is meant as praise for the craftsmen and as a warning to you. 

Then there's the CGI. The shots of a vast undersea tunnel work well, but the tentacles are another story. They have no mass, they seem to be lit for some other movie, and they look like exactly what they are. Gordon is smart enough to keep them out of frame as much as he can. But twenty years earlier, Andrzej Żuławski put a tentacled thing in a Berlin apartment in <span data-imdb-id="tt0082933">_Possession_</span>, and I believed every glistening inch of it.

The rain is what stays with me. Gordon has made better films, but none of them have this sense of a place that's been waiting under the water for a very long time. If you liked the earlier ones and can forgive the tentacles, the trip to Imboca is worth it.
