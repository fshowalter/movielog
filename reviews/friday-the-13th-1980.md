Sean S. Cunningham, who produced and directed _Friday the 13th_, and Victor Miller, who wrote it, had already made two kids' sports comedies because <span data-imdb-id="tt0074174">_The Bad News Bears_</span> made money. When <span data-imdb-id="tt0077651">_Halloween_</span> made more, they made a horror film. On the audio commentary Miller confesses he never cared for the genre and would rather have written <span data-imdb-id="tt0080339">_Airplane!_</span> Nobody was planning a franchise; they wanted to keep working.

The movie opens in 1958 at Camp Crystal Lake, where two counselors sneak away from a campfire singalong to make out and are murdered by someone we see only as a moving camera. Twenty-two years later, the camp the locals call Camp Blood is getting ready to reopen. New counselors arrive to paint cabins and fix the dock, and at night they play strip Monopoly, which I gather is how young people got through rainy evenings before smartphones. Then they start disappearing.

Anyone who knows the series by its hockey mask will be puzzled. There is no mask, and Jason is only the name of a boy who drowned at the camp in 1957. The killer's identity stays hidden until the last reel, so the movie plays as a whodunit. Dub the dialogue, put black gloves on the killer, and you could pass it off as an Italian giallo. Like the better gialli, it plays fair, with an early glimpse if you're quick. I missed it the first time I saw it. You may do better.

Miller adds Crazy Ralph, a local played by Walt Gorney, who pedals up to inform the kids they are doomed. A townsman calls him a prophet of doom, sparing us the trouble, and plenty of slashers over the next decade found work for a Ralph.

In 1996, <span data-imdb-id="tt0117571">_Scream_</span> laid out the rules of the genre, starting with no sex and no drinking or drugs. By those rules Alice, played by Adrienne King, should die first. The script hints she has had something going with the man who runs the camp, and she flirts with a fellow counselor and smokes dope with the rest of them. One couple is killed after sex. Most of the others die for going outside to check on a noise. Alice lives because she happens to, and that is what makes the movie frightening: bad things happen to ordinary kids for no apparent reason.

Cunningham filmed at a real Boy Scout camp in New Jersey at the end of the season, racing the turning leaves, and in a few shots the leaves win. But the earlier footage is green and damp, and he lingers over the lake and the woods longer than I remembered, giving the movie a reality the later sequels lost once they decamped to California. <span data-imdb-id="tt30321146">_In a Violent Nature_</span> recently stretched that kind of patience to feature length. The idea starts here.

The cast is mostly unknowns from off-Broadway, and they behave like summer employees, which is what they're playing. Cunningham's one familiar face was Betsy Palmer, for years a panelist on _I've Got a Secret_, and he could afford her for only a few days. The savings went to Tom Savini, who had done the makeup for George Romero's <span data-imdb-id="tt0077402">_Dawn of the Dead_</span>. His gore adds up to maybe a minute of screen time, and it holds up. The best of it goes to Kevin Bacon, sixth-billed, who is lying on a bunk when a hand reaches up from underneath to pin his head and an arrow comes through the mattress and out of his throat. Miller wrote it to act out every child's fear of the thing under the bed. Savini's makeup is seamless enough that you don't stop to wonder how long the killer has been down there, or what the plan was if Bacon had taken the other bunk.

Harry Manfredini borrows his shrieking strings from <span data-imdb-id="tt0054215">_Psycho_</span>, but the whispered "ki-ki-ki" is his own and has followed the series ever since. The rest of the score, with its horns, feels more traditional than the synthesized soundtracks about to take over the genre.

The last act wears thin. Cunningham leaves King alone onscreen for long stretches and pushes her into sobbing and screaming that exceed her range. Alice knocks the killer down, drops her weapon, runs off to hide somewhere else, whimpers, and waits to be found. Then she does it again. By the third round you may be giving advice to someone who can't hear you.

The epilogue nearly makes up for all of it. I'll say only that if you haven't seen it, you will jump. I don't care for jump scares, but this one sent audiences out talking and handed the sequels their killer. Cunningham and Miller kept working, though neither came back for <span data-imdb-id="tt0082418">_Part 2_</span>.
